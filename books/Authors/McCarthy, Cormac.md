# Cormac McCarthy

## One-line frame
McCarthy writes parable rather than psychological novel — characters are vessels for moral/existential propositions, not people to be developed. Prose stripped to bone, biblical cadence, no quotation marks. The apocalypse is total where Krasznahorkai's is municipal; the family bond is what survives when every system collapses.

## Dialectical partners
- [[Han, Byung-Chul]] — answers Han. Han says the self-exploiting subject is trapped alone. McCarthy (with Awad) answers: the exit is lateral, toward another person, through a bond the system can't reach. Not optimism — locating the structural weakness in a closed system.

## Parallels
- Hemingway — prose heir. Closest thing to Hemingway's heir in American prose. Same iceberg method, same refusal to explain, same trust in the reader. But darker — Hemingway hides sadness under precision; McCarthy hides horror.
- [[Awad, Mona]] — family as exit. Same conclusion through opposite methods. *Rouge*: hyper-civilization's trap, exit through mother-daughter love. *The Road*: no civilization at all, same irreducible family unit survives. Love as the positive feedback loop the system can't metabolize.
- Camus — carrying the fire. The father carrying the fire through a dead world IS Sisyphus pushing the boulder. No rational reason to continue. He continues. The absurd as endurance.
- King — archetypal vessels. Both use characters as vessels for moral/existential propositions, not psychological studies. *Long Walk* and *The Road*: people walking forward through landscapes trying to kill them. Endurance as the question.
- [[Sebald, W.G.]] — unrecoverable past. Sebald's emigrants can't go home because home changed. McCarthy's final paragraph: the past wasn't just changed, it was *annihilated*. Sebald's loss is displacement. McCarthy's is extinction.
- [[Ogawa, Yoko]] — world stripped bare. Both: worlds where things have been removed. Ogawa removes selectively, people adjust calmly. McCarthy removes everything, survival becomes the only question. Different scales of loss.
- [[Faulkner, William]] — American Southern lineage, though this is more setting than method. Faulkner writes impossibility of escape from family/history; McCarthy writes what remains when the exit arrives only as apocalypse.

## In tension with
- [[Fisher, Mark]] — end of world easier. *The Road* is literally what Fisher says is easier to imagine than the end of capitalism. McCarthy can write civilizational extinction; nobody in the network writes the end of the system.
- Psychological novel. The Road has two named characters (the man, the boy) and multiple unnamed others. This is method, not limitation — the absence of psychological interiority is what lets the parable operate.
- Academic McCarthy criticism that wants The Road to be about ecological collapse, American imperialism, theodicy, or the ethics of survival. My reading: the moral content is thin. Be good. Keep going. Carry the fire. That's it. No Dostoevskian tension between competing positions. The "good guys" are good. The bad guys are bad. Parable, not novel — and parables don't need complex morals.

## Central concepts
- [[Family as positive feedback loop]] — surfaces across McCarthy, Awad, Morrison. Not just that family saves you passively — caring generates more capacity to care. The father's love for the son gives him strength to walk; walking keeps the son alive; the son's existence gives the father reason. Remove either node and the loop collapses. Both *Rouge* and *The Road* show the loop *can* fail, which is what keeps it from being naive.
- *The fire as relay* — not a possession, a passing-forward. Existed before the father, the father carried it, the son carries it now, someone will carry it after. Nobody invented it, nobody owns it. The structure of culture itself — language, stories, values — passed through carriers who didn't create them and won't see where they end up. The inversion at the ending: the son was the father's god while the father lived; the father becomes the son's god at the moment of death. God creates man, man carries God forward, each sustains the other.
- *Municipal apocalypse vs. total apocalypse* — the edge with Krasznahorkai. Same structural question (how does a world end?), different scales. Krasznahorkai: decay as ambient permanent condition, the event already happened, no one noticed. McCarthy: extinction event, total, the world is gone.

## Form / style
Unpunctuated dialogue. Biblical rhythm. Refusal of explanation. Present-tense survival. The syntax enacts the barrenness of the landscape — nothing decorative survives. McCarthy's stripped style is doing something similar to what Krasznahorkai does from the opposite direction: Krasznahorkai buries you in endless sentences; McCarthy strips sentences to bone. Both achieve the same effect — the prose *is* the world.

No context, no backstory beyond fragments, no explanation for why the world ended. Operates entirely in present-tense survival. The absence of context is the point: the father and son don't have the luxury of understanding what happened. They just have the road.

## Notes
- *The Road* is short and fast — finished in a day or two. It hits hard and immediate, more like Schweblin's punch than Sebald's accumulation.
- The final paragraph is doing something similar to *Lot 49*'s ending — it gestures toward something that can't be reached. Pynchon gestures toward an answer that never arrives. McCarthy gestures toward a past that can't be recovered. Both endings point at absence. Opposite emotional temperatures — Pynchon's absence is intellectual vertigo, McCarthy's is grief.

## Open threads
- Whether *Blood Meridian* validates or complicates the "parable not psychological novel" reading. Some sources say *Blood Meridian* is where McCarthy's philosophy of evil actually lives, and *The Road* is a more accessible palimpsest over that deeper project. To be tested on next read.
- The "fire as relay" reading — whether McCarthy intends it as explicitly as the prose allows, or whether this is the reader's construction. Either way it's the most interesting structural move in the book.
- Whether the Hemingway genealogy holds across the full corpus or whether *The Road* is the specifically Hemingway-adjacent work and the earlier novels (especially *Blood Meridian*) are doing something else (more Melville, more Faulkner).
