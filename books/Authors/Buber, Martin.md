# Martin Buber

## One-line frame
Buber distinguishes two fundamental modes of relation — to a subject (*I-Thou*) and to an object (*I-It*) — and argues that modernity systematically expands the second at the expense of the first. He wrote this in 1923, before television, before the internet, before any of it. The prescience is uncanny and the framework is load-bearing.

## Dialectical partners
- Levinas (to be added) — Levinas extends and contests Buber, centering the face-to-face ethical encounter in a way Buber doesn't. Parallel project, different emphasis (relation vs. responsibility). Some readings insist Levinas is a correction rather than an extension.

## Parallels
- Dialogical philosophy more broadly — Buber is the pivotal figure but not the only one. The tradition runs through Rosenzweig, Marcel, Bakhtin.
- [[Cusk, Rachel]] — *Outline* as contemporary literary demonstration of I-Thou attention in prose rhythm. Cusk's listening is one of the few places where I-Thou still surfaces formally in recent fiction.

## In tension with
- Any philosophy that treats relation as a derivative property of independent substances. Buber's move is to make *I-Thou* prior — the self is constituted in relation, not before it.

## Central concepts
- [[I-Thou and I-It]] — the master distinction. Central to the [[AI Digital Terraforming]] thesis: what the digital environment systematically strips is exactly the capacity for *I-Thou*. Crucially, Buber does not say I-It is *bad* — he says it's necessary. But a world with *only* I-It is a world where genuine meeting has disappeared. That's exactly what digital terraforming produced: an environment optimized entirely for I-It, where I-Thou becomes rare and structurally unsupported.

## Form / style
*I and Thou* is a short, lyric, almost incantatory text — closer to liturgy than to analytic philosophy. Dense but not technical. This is deliberate: the book cannot argue for *I-Thou* without performing it, because *I-It* argumentation would undermine its own claim. The form is an argument about form.

## Open threads
- Whether Levinas is extension or correction — some readings push this hard.
- How the *I-Thou* / *I-It* distinction survives encounter with AI. Buber obviously did not address this, but the [[AI Digital Terraforming]] thesis has to work out whether an AI can be a *Thou* — or whether treating it as one is the category error that completes the terraforming.
- AI as the perfected I-It partner: never demands I-Thou, never confronts with genuine otherness, never risks, never refuses. The real horror is not that the machine replaces the human but that we can no longer tell the difference because we forgot what I-Thou felt like. Cusk's "more tenderness from a machine than from one's fellow man" is that moment captured.
