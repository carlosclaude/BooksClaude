# William Faulkner

## One-line frame
Faulkner treats the American South as an impossibility machine: a place where the past does not pass, trauma has no outside, and consciousness itself becomes the structure of captivity. The four-voice architecture of *The Sound and the Fury* is not four perspectives on one event — it is four incompatible ontologies of the same family.

## Dialectical partners
- [[Cusk, Rachel]] — structural inverse. Cusk uses absence as viable state; Faulkner treats escape as unavailable. Faye vanishes from her own life and keeps functioning. The Compsons cannot vanish and cannot function. Same diagnosis of the self collapsing under family weight, opposite prescriptions.

## Parallels
- McCarthy — another American Southerner writing impossible exits. *The Road* works through what Faulkner implies: once the outside also collapses, the family bond is the only remaining structure.
- [[Pynchon, Thomas]] — prose momentum. Both write sentences that accumulate and refuse settlement. Different projects, shared rhythm.
- DFW — spotted a Nastasya–Caddy structural parallel across traditions. The woman-as-obsession whose unavailability organizes male consciousness runs through both Faulkner and Dostoevsky; DFW noticed it and wrote about it explicitly.

## In tension with
- Novelistic realism generally. *The Sound and the Fury* is not a novel of social observation; it is a novel of four incompatible ontologies.

## Central concepts
- [[Family as positive feedback loop]] — surfaces across Faulkner, McCarthy, Morrison. In Faulkner the family is the trap that also preserves: you can't escape it and can't live without it.

## Form / style
Stream of consciousness as structure, not technique. The Benjy section is not a *representation* of confusion — it *is* confusion, navigable only through sensory-trigger association (smell → Caddy → memory → present, in any order, no index). The four voices of *The Sound and the Fury* move from maximum disorientation (Benjy) to circular obsession (Quentin) to bitter pragmatism (Jason) to outside third-person order (Dilsey).

My actual reading of Sound: Quentin is tragic — crushed by a Southern code of honor that no longer exists, obsessed with Caddy's virginity as an abstract concept rather than his actual sister. The broken-watch scene is the novel in miniature: he tries to literally destroy time because he cannot bear things changing and degrading. Jason is the most detestable character and the most "functional" in appearance — Caroline (the mother) fed him resentment and entitlement. Quentin Jr. taking the money and escaping at the end is the only real agency in the whole family; she breaks the cycle.

Sensory obsession is the cross-voice constant. Benjy processes the world almost entirely through smell — "Caddy smelled like trees" is his organizing principle. But the motif extends: the whole family's decay can almost literally be *smelled* across the book.

## Notes
- The 1945 Compson Appendix closes enormous interpretive distance. Reading it after the novel (not during) is essential — it's what Faulkner himself added to clarify what he had deliberately refused to clarify in 1929. Tension between the appendix and the refusal is its own interpretive problem.
- Vintage 2025 "Complete and Definitive" edition includes the Appendix.

## Open threads
- Does *Absalom, Absalom!* extend the *Sound and the Fury* project or displace it? Same county, different method — unclear yet whether to read as sequel or correction.
- The "positive feedback loop" reading of the Compson family may be more mine than Faulkner's. Worth testing on *Absalom* and *Light in August* to see if it holds across the corpus or was specific to one family.
- Whether the four-voice method is repeatable or a one-time event. *As I Lay Dying* uses multiple voices but differently (15 of them, flatter). Sound is the more radical move.
