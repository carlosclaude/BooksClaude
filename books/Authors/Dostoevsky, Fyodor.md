# Fyodor Dostoevsky

## One-line frame
Dostoevsky stages the collision between modern rational self-understanding and its own remainders — guilt, faith, freedom, suffering — as polyphonic novels in which no voice, including the author's, is authoritative. Origin node for most of the philosophical-fiction layer.

## Dialectical partners
- [[Nietzsche, Friedrich]] — the defining dialectical pair. Not parallel, not tension — *dialogue*. Same diagnosis of bourgeois comfort as lie; same recognition that suffering is constitutive of being human rather than an accident. Divergent prescriptions: Dostoevsky says go deeper into suffering until you hit transcendence (Sonya's grace, Alyosha's kiss). Nietzsche says go deeper until you transform into something that no longer needs the transcendent (amor fati, the Übermensch). Same descent, different destination — each destination only makes sense because the other exists as contrast. Nietzsche named Dostoevsky "the only psychologist from whom I had something to learn." The dialogue exists entirely across their works; they never met.

## Parallels
- Tolstoy — the other great Russian novelist, radically different project. Tolstoy: moral vision clarified through narrative. Dostoevsky: no clarification possible, only voices. Tolstoy has an author-God; Dostoevsky does not.
- [[Kundera, Milan]] — *The Gambler* ↔ *The Joke*. Same architecture: a single gesture triggers total destruction of a life. But Dostoevsky's world has too much meaning (every act loaded with metaphysical weight), Kundera's has too little (the joke means nothing, and that's what destroys Ludvik).
- [[Kafka, Franz]] — alienation lineage. The Underground Man becoming Gregor Samsa: alienation first internalized, then made literal. Kafka extends Dostoevsky's psychology by turning it into metamorphosis.
- [[Krasznahorkai, László]] — provincial darkness. Provincial towns as laboratories for human darkness. The Hungarian town in *Melancholy of Resistance* is a Hungarian version of Dostoevsky's small-town hells. Fascism emerging from ordinary desperation, not ideology.
- Steinbeck — *East of Eden* as *The Brothers Karamazov* transplanted to California. Cain and Abel, the question of whether humans can choose good over evil, the father's shadow. Dostoevsky's moral architecture inside Salinas Valley light.
- Dazai — Japanese branch. *No Longer Human* is *Notes from Underground* transposed into post-war Japanese shame culture.
- PKD — paranoia lineage. Dostoevsky's paranoia rewritten as sci-fi epistemology ("what if reality is fake?").
- Stendhal — *The Red and the Black* is the prototype. Julien Sorel gets there first (1830); Raskolnikov (1866) inherits the architecture. Stendhal diagnoses the social mechanics of ambition; Dostoevsky fills the same structure with metaphysical crisis. Stendhal diagnoses; Dostoevsky agonizes.

## In tension with
- Utilitarian and rationalist ethics — *Notes from Underground* is the foundational polemic against these. The Underground Man's whole point is that rational self-interest cannot describe what a person actually is.
- Easy Christian reading of his own work — Dostoevsky is Christian, but the novels are not apologetics. The Grand Inquisitor chapter is the clearest case: the force of the atheistic argument is left fully intact. Ivan's position is as compelling as Alyosha's, which is what makes the novel work.
- DFW ("The serious writer avoiding ideology") pointed out that today the pressure is different: not state ideology but market and social pressure for legibility. Dostoevsky's resistance was conviction-based and easier than resistance now would be.

## Central concepts
- *Polyphony* (via Bakhtin's reading) — the formal principle of multiple unmerged voices. No voice, including the narrator's, given hierarchical authority over the others. This is what distinguishes Dostoevsky from the 19th-century realist tradition he's often placed within.
- *Underground* / the underground man — consciousness as a disease.
- The Grand Inquisitor problem — freedom as burden, not gift. Most politically prescient passage in 19th-century fiction.

## Form / style
Polyphonic in Bakhtin's technical sense. Plot is excessive, melodramatic, implausible — this is the method, not a flaw. The extremity is required to force the ideas into action.

Translations matter. Pevear/Volokhonsky is now the standard; earlier Constance Garnett versions read smoother but flatten the polyphony.

## Notes
- The Siberian years (*The House of the Dead*) are not optional. The post-exile work is unreadable without them.
- Origin node in the Three.js network — more outgoing edges than almost any other author. That centrality is not inflation; it's accurate to the influence.
