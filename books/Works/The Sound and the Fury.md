# The Sound and the Fury

**by [[Faulkner, William]]**

## Thesis
The Compson family cannot end because no one in it can narrate an ending. The novel's four voices are not perspectives on a shared event; they are four incompatible ontologies of the same decay, and their incompatibility is the point.

## Structure / form
Four sections, each a different consciousness:
- **Benjy** (1928) — no time, no understanding of time, navigates entirely by sensory-trigger association
- **Quentin** (1910) — the opposite problem: understands time so completely it destroys him
- **Jason** (1928) — the first "legible" voice, also the cruelest; narrative clarity as moral flatness
- **Dilsey** (1928) — external third person, the only voice that sees the whole

The sequence matters. Benjy is maximally disoriented but emotionally innocent. Jason is maximally clear and morally bankrupt. Dilsey arrives as the only perspective from outside the Compson system.

## What's actually happening
Aristocratic Mississippi family in terminal decline. The three Compson sons — one intellectually disabled (Benjy), one suicidal (Quentin), one bitter and grasping (Jason) — orbit their sister Caddy, who has been exiled from the family after a pregnancy out of wedlock. The novel is set across two days in 1928 with a long flashback to 1910.

## My reading
Benjy and Quentin are not a contrast between "confusion" and "clarity." They are two ways of being trapped in time — one by not understanding it at all, one by understanding it too completely. Faulkner's point is that the family structure consumes both kinds of mind equally.

The "positive feedback loop" reading: the Compson family is self-preserving in the worst possible sense. No outside intervenes; the system runs itself. This is what connects to McCarthy's *The Road* and Morrison's *Beloved* — the family as the last remaining social unit once everything else has collapsed, but also the thing that will not let anyone out.

Caddy as Benjy's only anchor — "Caddy smelled like trees" — is the sensory engine of his section. When she is exiled, he does not lose *her*; he loses the organizing principle of his consciousness. The 1910 flashback and the 1928 present are both about her absence.

## Key passages
- Opening of Benjy's section: the golf-course scene with "caddie" overheard as "Caddy" is the novel's method in miniature. A sensory trigger collapses decades.

## Open questions
- Whether the four sections can be read as an argument or only as a demonstration. An argument would have a thesis; Faulkner gives four voices and refuses synthesis. Dilsey does *some* synthesis work but leaves Benjy and Quentin still in their separate prisons.
- The 1945 Compson Appendix — does it complete the novel or contradict its refusal to resolve?
